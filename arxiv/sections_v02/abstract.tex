Small lesions in brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are easily missed: a segmentation model can reach a high Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) while leaving many of them undetected. We ask whether this omission comes from the network or from the training objective. In multiple sclerosis lesion segmentation, nnU-Net trained with its default Dice and cross-entropy loss produces almost no response to most of the small lesions it misses, so no decision threshold recovers them, and four other architectures trained with the same loss detect no more of them. We therefore change only the objective. CATMIL adds two lesion-level terms to the nnU-Net loss: a Component-Adaptive Tversky (CAT) term, which gives each lesion a weight nearly independent of its size, and a Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) term, which treats each lesion as a bag of voxels and penalizes any lesion in which no voxel is detected. On MSLesSeg, CATMIL raises small-lesion recall from 0.796 to 0.873 (difference 95\% CI +0.030 to +0.157) and misses 48\% fewer lesions, at comparable DSC. The gain holds on the cross-validation predictions of 47 further patients, for lesions of at least 3~mm, and on a second dataset trained with the same loss weights. CATMIL also detects more small lesions than CC-DiceCE, an instance-level loss that balances lesions within an overlap term (0.873 vs.\ 0.831), indicating that lesions must be supervised not only in proportion but also for their detection. The cost is additional small false-positive components; a component-size filter removes most of them, and at the lesion-wise precision of the default loss CATMIL still detects more small lesions.
