This work asked why segmentation models miss small lesions in brain MRI.
In MS lesion segmentation, standard objectives produce almost no response
to most of the small lesions they miss, and the architectures we tested do
not change this when trained with the same objective. The omission arises
in training, from how supervision is distributed across lesions.

CATMIL supervises each lesion as a unit in two ways: the CAT term balances
the overlap signal across lesions of different sizes, and the MIL term
penalizes every lesion in which no voxel is detected. Together they detect
more small lesions than losses that only reweight voxels or balance
lesions within an overlap term, with the detection gain carried mainly by
the MIL term. The gain holds
across patients, on a larger cross-validation sample, for lesions of
clinically reportable size, and on a second dataset trained with the same
loss weights, and it does not require any change to the network.

The cost is additional small false-positive components. They can be
removed after training, and at the precision of the standard loss CATMIL
still detects more small lesions, but the filter size depends on the data,
and against Tversky-based losses part of the gain reflects a more
sensitive operating point. CATMIL is therefore most useful where a missed
lesion is a more serious error than a small false positive. Separating
detection from delineation in the objective suggests two next steps: an
analogous term that penalizes unmatched predicted components, and
lesion-level supervision of complete delineation rather than of a single
detected voxel.
