Small lesions in brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are sparse targets
embedded in a large volume of normal-appearing tissue. In multiple
sclerosis (MS), the number of lesions and the appearance of new lesions on
follow-up scans are central to diagnosis~\cite{barkhof_2024_2025} and to
the monitoring of disease activity~\cite{wattjes_2021_2021}, so a small
lesion that is never detected is a clinically relevant error, even when it
barely changes the segmented volume.

Progress in medical image segmentation has come largely from architectures
and pipelines: U-Net and its 3D extensions~\cite{navab_u-net_2015}, the
self-configuring nnU-Net~\cite{isensee_nnu-net_2021}, and transformer-based
and hybrid models~\cite{hatamizadeh_unetr_2021,hatamizadeh_swin_2022}. Yet
small lesions are still missed by state-of-the-art models; in the
nnU-Net-based FLAMeS, most missed lesions were smaller than
10~mm$^3$~\cite{dereskewicz_flames_2025}. This raises a question that
architectural progress does not answer: are small lesions missed because
the network cannot represent them, or because the training objective gives
it no reason to?

The objective is a plausible cause. In a voxel-wise loss, a lesion of a
few voxels is a negligible part of the objective, so a model can reach a
high Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) while ignoring it. Losses for class
imbalance reweight voxels, and instance-level losses balance lesions of
different sizes, but both still judge each lesion by how much of it is
overlapped. Neither asks the more basic question of whether the lesion is
detected at all, and no existing objective supervises this separately from
delineation (\cref{sec:related}).

We test this explanation by changing only the objective. CATMIL adds two
lesion-level terms to the standard nnU-Net loss, one for each property
that a voxel-wise loss lacks: a \emph{Component-Adaptive Tversky (CAT)}
term, which gives small and large lesions comparable weight, and a
\emph{Multiple Instance Learning (MIL)} term, which penalizes any lesion in
which no voxel is detected. Because the network is fixed, differences in
behavior can be attributed to the supervision. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A diagnosis of how small lesions are missed.} Standard losses
do not miss small lesions by a narrow margin: the network produces almost
no response to most of them, so the omission cannot be corrected by the
decision threshold. On MSLesSeg, four other architectures trained with the
same loss detect no more small lesions, whereas changing only the
objective recovers many of them.
\item \textbf{A lesion-level detection objective for fully supervised
segmentation.} To our knowledge, CATMIL is the first use of multiple
instance learning as a per-lesion existence constraint when voxel-level
annotations are available. We show analytically that the CAT term gives
each lesion a total weight nearly independent of its size and that the MIL
term gives every lesion the same detection signal, largest when the lesion
is missed. The objective requires no change to the network.
\item \textbf{Evidence that lesion-level supervision recovers the missed
lesions.} CATMIL detects more small lesions than losses that reweight
voxels or balance lesions within an overlap term, including the
instance-level loss CC-DiceCE, and an ablation separates the roles of its
two terms. The gain holds across patients, on 47
cross-validation patients, for lesions above the size used in clinical
reading, and on a second dataset trained with the same loss weights.
\item \textbf{A characterization of the cost.} The added sensitivity
produces small false-positive components. They can be removed after
training, and at the lesion-wise precision of the standard loss CATMIL
still detects more small lesions; against Tversky-based losses, part of
the gain reflects a more sensitive operating point.
\end{itemize}
