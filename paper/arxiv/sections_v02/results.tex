The results are organized around the question posed in the introduction.
\Cref{sec:main_results} establishes the main effect, \cref{sec:where_gain}
and \cref{sec:no_response} show where the gain arises and how the
baselines fail, \cref{sec:arch_vs_objective} and \cref{sec:ablation}
attribute the failure to the objective and separate the roles of the two
terms,
\cref{sec:fp_pp} characterizes the cost, and \cref{sec:robustness} tests
whether the findings hold beyond the six MSLesSeg test patients.

\Cref{tab:loss_comparison_small_lesion,tab:paired_differences} give the
main comparison. Three observations frame the rest of the section.

\textbf{CATMIL detects more small lesions than every compared loss.} On
MSLesSeg, it raises small-lesion recall from 0.796 with DiceCE to 0.873,
and misses 48\% fewer lesions per case. The gain is present in all six test
patients against every baseline, with CIs that exclude zero
(\cref{tab:paired_differences}). The three alternatives that already
emphasize false negatives or individual lesions, Tversky, FocalTversky, and
CC-DiceCE, all reach a similar intermediate level (0.831--0.834). The
gain does not rely on the lenient any-overlap detection criterion: when at
least half of a lesion must be covered, the gain over DiceCE remains about
8 percentage points on MSLesSeg and 10 on 3D-MR-MS
(\cref{appendix:strict-detection}).

\textbf{The recovered lesions do not come at the cost of overlap or of
lesion volume.} The DSC and HD95 of CATMIL are within the variation between
patients of every baseline on MSLesSeg, and its false-positive volume is no
larger than that of any baseline. CATMIL therefore does not find more
lesions by predicting more lesion tissue. Equally, the DSC barely registers
the recovered lesions, which is the gap between voxel- and lesion-level
evaluation that motivates this work (\cref{eq:sl_missed_lesion}).

\textbf{The cost appears in lesion-wise F1.} CATMIL has the lowest
lesion-wise F1 in every test patient. Together with its unchanged
false-positive volume, this implies many small false-positive components
rather than over-segmentation, which \cref{sec:fp_pp} confirms.

\subsection{The Gain Is a Net Gain, Concentrated Where the Baselines Fail}
\label{sec:where_gain}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/recall_vs_size_curve.pdf}
    \caption{Lesion recall against lesion size on the MSLesSeg test set.
    Upper panel: fraction of reference lesions detected in each size
    interval (logarithmic axis); lines are the mean over the five fold
    models and shaded bands span the fold minimum to maximum. Lower panel:
    number of reference lesions per interval. Dotted vertical lines mark
    10, 50, and 200~mm$^3$. A lesion counts as detected if any predicted
    voxel overlaps it.}
    \label{fig:recall_vs_size_curve}
\end{figure}

\textbf{CATMIL moves the detection limit toward smaller lesions.}
\Cref{fig:recall_vs_size_curve} shows that the gap between CATMIL and the
baselines is widest exactly where the baselines fail, between about 5 and
50~mm$^3$, and closes above about 100~mm$^3$, where every loss detects
nearly all lesions. One way to summarize the shift is the size at which
recall reaches 0.8: about 40~mm$^3$ for DiceCE, 20--25~mm$^3$ for the
Tversky-based losses and CC-DiceCE, and about 13~mm$^3$ for CATMIL. Lesions
of at most 5~mm$^3$ remain difficult for every loss, so the size bias of
voxel-wise training is reduced but not removed.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/lesion_fate.pdf}

    lesions detected by both or by neither. Values are means over the five

    \label{fig:lesion_fate}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The gain is not a redistribution of errors.} A higher recall could
hide a trade in which CATMIL gains some lesions but loses others.
\Cref{fig:lesion_fate} pairs every reference lesion between CATMIL and each
baseline trained on the same fold, and the exchange is strongly one-sided:
per fold model, CATMIL detects 30.6 lesions that DiceCE misses, while
DiceCE detects 2.0 that CATMIL misses, and the ratio against CC-DiceCE is
15.4 to 2.4. No lesion above 200~mm$^3$ is lost. The same holds across
cases (\cref{fig:fn_per_case_paired}): against DiceCE and CC-DiceCE, CATMIL
misses fewer lesions in 10 of the 12 test cases and more in none.

\textbf{The gain is largest in patients with many small lesions, and the
DSC does not see it.} Most of the recovered lesions come from three
high-burden cases with many small lesions
(\cref{tab:test_case_lesion_profile}). In one of them, P47\_T1, CATMIL
recovers about 11 lesions relative to DiceCE while the case-level DSC stays
at 0.85: the recovered lesions are too small to move a volume-overlap
score. This is the failure mode that \cref{eq:sl_missed_lesion} predicts,
observed on real data.

\subsection{The Baselines Do Not Respond to the Lesions They Miss}
\label{sec:no_response}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/max_prob_by_size.pdf}
    \caption{Maximum foreground probability inside each reference lesion on
    the MSLesSeg test set, by lesion size. Bars give the fraction of
    lesions whose maximum probability is below 0.1 (no response), between
    0.1 and 0.5 (weak response below the threshold), or at least 0.5
    (detected). Each lesion contributes once per fold model; $n$ is the
    number of reference lesions in the bin.}
    \label{fig:max_prob_by_size}
\end{figure}

A missed lesion can be missed narrowly, with a response just below the
decision threshold, or entirely, with no response at all. The distinction
matters: the first can be corrected at inference, the second only during
training. \Cref{fig:max_prob_by_size} assigns the maximum foreground
probability inside every reference lesion to one of three levels.

\textbf{The baselines miss small lesions entirely.} For lesions of at most
10~mm$^3$, DiceCE assigns a maximum probability below 0.1 to 82\% of them,
and even the best baseline, CC-DiceCE, to 62\%. The intermediate level,
a weak response below the threshold, never exceeds 8\% for any loss. A
lower threshold therefore cannot recover these lesions; the omission is
already present in the network output. CATMIL reduces the fraction with no
response to 47\%. It does not merely push weak responses over the
threshold: it makes the network respond to lesions that the other
objectives leave invisible. The same pattern holds on 3D-MR-MS
(\cref{fig:max_prob_by_size_3dmrms}).

    \label{fig:qual_lesion_gallery}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/qual_probability_maps.pdf}

\textbf{Individual lesions show the same mechanism.}
\Cref{fig:qual_lesion_gallery,fig:qual_probability_maps} show lesions
selected by a fixed rule rather than by visual inspection: a
\emph{recovered} lesion has at most 50 voxels, is detected by at least four
of the five CATMIL models, and by at most one model of each baseline; a
lesion \emph{missed by all} is detected by none of the twenty models; the
\emph{CATMIL false positive} is produced by all five CATMIL models and by no
baseline model. (The case analysis predates the CC-DiceCE experiments and
covers the other four losses.) Four observations stand out.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The omission is a size effect, not a visibility effect.} The
13-voxel lesion in row~2 has median contrast and is clearly visible on
FLAIR, but it is isolated, and all fifteen baseline models assign it a
probability of at most 0.01. It accounts for 0.07\% of the lesion voxels
of its case, but for one of its 59 lesions; only a lesion-level term gives
it a share of the training signal that matches the latter.
\item \emph{CATMIL detects, but does not fully delineate.} CATMIL detects
each recovered lesion in all five models with $p \geq 0.93$, but covers
only part of it (lesion-wise DSC 0.28--0.52) and in row~1 merges a
5-voxel lesion with a neighbor 3~mm away. The detection term decides
whether a lesion is found; its extent still depends on the overlap terms.
\item \emph{Some recovered lesions are clinically informative.} The
juxtacortical lesion in row~4 lies against bright cortex and is detected
by at most one baseline model; juxtacortical location is one of the
criteria for dissemination in space in
MS~\cite{thompson_diagnosis_2018}.
\item \emph{The remaining misses and the false positive reflect the image,
not the loss.} The lesion missed by all models (row~5) has a contrast below
that of 99\% of the small test lesions, and across the test set the small
lesions that CATMIL still misses have about half the contrast of those it
detects (median 1.86 against 3.65). The false positive (row~6) is a 3-voxel
focal hyperintensity with no annotated counterpart at follow-up: the same
response to compact bright foci that recovers rows~1--4, at a location the
reference does not mark.
\end{itemize}
Lesion characteristics and the contrast measure are described in
\cref{appendix:test-case-lesion-profile}.

\textbf{None of the architectures detected more small lesions than

measurably, and it was worse. Changing the objective instead raised recall

CIs excluding zero, and the gain remained after PP5.

DSC of nnU-Net and had a lower HD95, yet detected no more small lesions. A
network can thus be competitive on voxel-level metrics while leaving the
same lesions undetected. Across the convolutional, transformer, and
state-space designs tested, the omission of small lesions depended more on
how supervision is allocated across lesions than on the network that
receives it. These architectures were not tuned individually, and CATMIL
was not combined with them, so this result shows that the tested changes of
architecture do not substitute for a change of objective, not that
architecture is irrelevant.

\textbf{The two terms act on different properties of the prediction.} The
MIL term carries the detection gain: alone, it raises small-lesion recall
by as much as CATMIL, and it also accounts for the lower lesion-wise F1, so
the additional false-positive components (\cref{sec:fp_pp}) are a
by-product of the detection signal. The CAT term alone moves small-lesion
recall in the same direction, close to the level of the other losses that
balance or reweight overlap (Tversky, FocalTversky, and CC-DiceCE,
0.831--0.834), though without a measurable difference from DiceCE and without
lowering lesion-wise F1. Combined, the two terms give the highest recall
together with the best mean DSC and HD95 of the three variants. This is
the division of labor the objective was designed for: the MIL term ensures
that each lesion is found, and the CAT term supervises the overlap of
lesions of different sizes in proportion.

\textbf{On this test set, the contribution of the CAT term is not
measurable.} CATMIL and DiceCE\,+\,MIL do not differ on any metric
(small-lesion recall +0.002 [$-0.012$, +0.019]; HD95 $-1.26$~mm
[$-6.82$, +1.55]). The loss-weight analysis shows that the ratio of the
two weights shifts the balance between voxel-level accuracy and
lesion-wise precision (\cref{appendix:catmil-loss-weights}), but those
runs are single models. Whether the CAT term contributes measurably to
delineation, or on data with a different lesion-size distribution, remains
to be tested.

\subsection{The Cost: Small False-Positive Components}
\label{sec:fp_pp}

\textbf{The false positives are many, small, and near true lesions.}
Without filtering, CATMIL produces 20.3 false-positive components per case
on MSLesSeg, against 5.4--7.0 for the voxel-level losses and 11.2 for
CC-DiceCE (\cref{appendix:lesion-level-analyses}). Yet its false-positive
volume is no larger than that of any baseline, and 70--80\% of the
false-positive voxels lie within a median of 1--2~mm of a reference lesion.
The cost is thus a set of small fragments and lesion-like foci, not large
false alarms. This is consistent with the ablation: a term that rewards
any confident voxel inside a lesion also lowers the threshold for
responding to small lesion-like structures elsewhere.

\textbf{Because the components are small, a size filter removes most of
them after training.} Removing predicted components smaller than 5 voxels
(PP5) raises CATMIL's lesion-wise F1 from 0.757 to 0.842, against gains of
0.013--0.015 for the voxel-level losses, while small-lesion recall
decreases only from 0.873 to 0.855 and remains the highest
(\cref{tab:loss_comparison_small_lesion_pp5} in
\cref{appendix:lesion-level-analyses}). After PP5 the lesion-wise F1
difference is no longer supported against any baseline on the test set,
whereas the recall gain remains in all six patients
(\cref{tab:paired_differences}). On the larger cross-validation sample,
however, a small F1 cost after PP5 remains (\cref{sec:catmil_oof}).

\subsection{Robustness Beyond the Six Test Patients}
\label{sec:robustness}

The MSLesSeg test set has six patients. Three further analyses test
whether the findings depend on them: a larger sample from the same
dataset, a clinically defined lesion size, and a second dataset.

\subsubsection{A Second Dataset: 3D-MR-MS}
\label{sec:3dmrms}

3D-MR-MS was acquired at a different center with a finer, anisotropic
resolution and a much larger share of very small lesions, and all five
losses were trained on it with the settings fixed on MSLesSeg.

\textbf{The detection gain transfers.} CATMIL again detects the most small
lesions (0.618 against 0.537 for DiceCE) and misses 23\% fewer lesions,
in five of the six patients against every baseline
(\cref{tab:loss_comparison_small_lesion,tab:paired_differences}).
CC-DiceCE is again the closest competitor: CATMIL misses fewer lesions
than it (2.27 per case, CI excluding zero), although the small-lesion
recall difference (+0.039) narrowly includes zero. The lesion-level
analyses reproduce the mechanism: the exchange of detected lesions with
DiceCE is 33 to 2.4 per fold model, and the fraction of lesions of at most
10~mm$^3$ with no response falls from 57--58\% to 39\%
(\cref{appendix:lesion-level-analyses}).

\textbf{The cost is larger, and the five-voxel filter does not suffice.}
Without filtering, CATMIL produces about 150 false-positive components per
case, against 14--19 for the voxel-level losses, and its lesion-wise F1 is
the lowest in every patient. Its DSC is also slightly lower than that of
DiceCE ($-0.012$) in all six patients, so on this dataset the DSC is
slightly reduced rather than preserved. After PP5, the F1 gap narrows but
remains against the voxel-level losses, and the recall advantage survives
only against DiceCE. The false-positive components on this dataset are
small in voxels rather than in millimeters, and a 20-voxel filter brings
CATMIL to the false-positive count of DiceCE with slightly higher recall,
but this setting was chosen after seeing the data. One test patient
contributes 249 of the 389 reference lesions, so pooled lesion counts on
this dataset are dominated by that patient, whereas the patient-level
analysis weights patients equally.
